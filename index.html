Non-Attachment in Buddhism

Featured reflection · April 2026

Seeing the video through the lens of non-attachment

In Buddhist thought, non-attachment (Pali: nekkhamma, Sanskrit: anupādāna) is not indifference or emotional withdrawal. It is the practice of meeting experience fully while loosening the grip of clinging — to objects, to outcomes, and to a fixed sense of self. The teaching rests on the recognition that all conditioned things are impermanent (anicca), that clinging to what changes produces suffering (dukkha), and that no experience contains a permanent, separate self (anattā).

Watched in this light, the video becomes a small laboratory for awareness. Notice the pull toward wanting the speaker to continue, to agree, to conclude in a particular way. Notice the quiet aversion when a point lands uncomfortably. Each of these movements of mind is an invitation — not to suppress the reaction, but to see it clearly and let it pass, the way a cloud crosses an open sky.

Three pointers for practice

  1. Receive, don't grasp. Let images and ideas arrive without reaching after them. If a phrase moves you, feel it; then release it rather than rehearsing it into identity.
  2. Watch the watcher. Non-attachment also loosens attachment to the observer. Who is the one pleased, bored, convinced? The question dissolves a subtle form of clinging.
  3. Carry the stillness out. The test of the teaching is not in the stillness of the cushion or the screen, but in how you meet the next conversation, message, or disappointment.
“You only lose what you cling to.” — attributed to the Buddha

Non-attachment is not the end of caring. It is caring without the weight of possession — a way of being with the world that is both warmer and lighter than the mind's usual grip.
