Non-Attachment in Buddhism

Featured reflection · April 2026

What the video argues

The video braids together two strands: Robert Monroe's claim, from decades of out-of-body research, that human emotional intensity is being harvested as an energetic output he called loosh; and the Gnostic texts — the Apocryphon of John, the Hypostasis of the Archons, the Pistis Sophia — which describe a lower creator (Yaldabaoth) and his administrators (archons) who maintain a reality calibrated to produce exactly this output. The richest yield, both sources allegedly agree, is not raw pain but attachment: love shadowed by loss, hope shadowed by fear, longing that never quite resolves. The way out the video points toward is not more information but gnosis — a direct recognition of what, in us, is untouched by the machinery.

Reading it through Buddhist non-attachment

Whether or not one accepts the cosmology, the psychological map overlaps strikingly with core Buddhist teaching. Non-attachment (Pali nekkhamma, Sanskrit anupādāna) is not indifference. It is the practice of meeting experience fully while loosening the grip of clinging — to objects, outcomes, and a fixed sense of self. It rests on three recognitions: all conditioned things are impermanent (anicca); clinging to what changes produces suffering (dukkha); and no experience contains a permanent, separate self (anattā).

In this light, the video's "counterfeit spirit" looks very much like what the Buddha called taṇhā — the compulsive thirst that fabricates an "I" out of each pleasant or unpleasant feeling and then defends it. Whether we frame the mechanism as archons farming emotion or as the twelve links of dependent origination (paṭiccasamuppāda), the operational instruction is the same: clinging is the fuel; stop feeding it and the wheel slows.

Where Buddhism gently corrects the frame

  • No cosmic enemy is required. The Buddha was explicit that speculation about hidden rulers of the world is a distraction from the present task. Suffering, its cause, its cessation, and the path are sufficient (cattāri ariyasaccāni).
  • Outrage at being "farmed" is itself clinging. The video notes this; Buddhism is unambiguous about it. Aversion (dosa) to one's situation binds the mind as tightly as craving. Even the thought "I have been used" is a construction the mind can release.
  • Gnosis is not a secret; it is seeing. Vipassanā — clear seeing — does not add knowledge. It subtracts the habit of identification. What remains is not a hidden spark to be retrieved but the already-present nature of awareness (in the Mahāyāna, buddhadhātu).
  • Compassion completes it. Where the Gnostic framing can tilt toward escape, Buddhist non-attachment stays engaged. The bodhisattva does not leave the "farm"; they remain in it, unclinging, for the benefit of others.

Three pointers for practice

  1. Meet the emotion, don't feed the story. When grief, fear, or longing arises, feel it bodily. Do not rehearse the narrative that makes it "yours." The charge passes; the story is what binds it.
  2. Watch the watcher. Ask who is outraged, who is enlightened, who has "seen through the system." That inquiry dissolves the subtle clinging the video warns about — psychic-gnosticism turning hidden knowledge into identity.
  3. Carry the stillness out. The measure of the teaching is not the quiet of the cushion or the thrill of the screen, but how you meet the next message, the next disappointment, the next person who needs you.
“You only lose what you cling to.” — attributed to the Buddha

Read through non-attachment, the video's most radical claim becomes its most ordinary: whatever is harvesting the emotional output, the exit is the same one the Buddha pointed to 2,500 years ago. Not better defences, not deeper cosmology — the simple, unglamorous release of the grip.
